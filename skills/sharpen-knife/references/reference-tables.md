# Reference Tables — sharpen-knife

Detailed reference tables extracted from `SKILL.md` for progressive disclosure.

## Blade Assessment & Light Test

| Condition | Signs | Action Needed |
|---|---|---|
| Dull (most common) | Won't catch on fingernail; slides off tomato skin; reflects light along edge | Medium grit -> fine grit -> strop |
| Very dull / abused | Visible flat spot along edge; tears rather than cuts; edge shines under light | Coarse grit -> medium -> fine -> strop |
| Nicked / chipped | Visible notches in edge; snags on material when drawing across | Coarse grit to grind past nicks -> reprofile -> strop |
| Slightly dull (maintenance) | Catches on fingernail but not crisply; still cuts paper but not cleanly | Strop only (or a few passes on fine grit then strop) |
| Rolled edge | Edge feels sharp on one side but dull on the other; blade curves microscopically | Strop firmly on both sides; if that fails, light passes on fine stone |

**Light Test:** Hold the blade edge-on under a bright light. A sharp edge is invisible — it has no width. A dull edge reflects a thin line of light where metal has folded or flattened.

## Abrasive Types & Grit Progression

| Type | Characteristics | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| Water stones (natural/synthetic) | Soak 5–15 min before use; fast cutting; wear quickly; need flattening periodically | Home sharpening; best feedback and finest edges |
| Diamond plates | No soaking needed (use water as lubricant); very durable; aggressive cut | Field use; hard steels; flattening water stones |
| Ceramic stones/rods | No soaking; very fine grit; hard and slow-wearing | Touch-up and maintenance; field carry |
| Oil stones (Arkansas, India) | Use honing oil; slower cutting; very durable; less messy | Traditional; workshop use |
| Field stones (improvised) | Any smooth, fine-grained stone (river stones, slate, sandstone); unpredictable grit | Wilderness emergency when no other abrasive available |

| Grit Range | Purpose | When to Use |
|---|---|---|
| 220–400 | Coarse | Reprofiling, removing chips, establishing a new bevel |
| 800–1000 | Medium | Standard sharpening of a dull blade; the workhorse grit |
| 3000–6000 | Fine | Refining the edge after medium; polishing the bevel |
| 8000+ | Ultra-fine | Mirror polish; razors; optional for most knives |

## Common Bevel Angles & Coin Stack Reference

| Knife Type | Angle (per side) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bushcraft / fixed blade | 20–25° | Balance of sharpness and durability for wood, rope, and general camp tasks |
| Kitchen knife | 15–20° | Thinner for clean food cuts; less durable on hard items |
| Japanese kitchen | 10–15° | Very acute; exceptional sharpness; fragile on bone |
| Machete / chopper | 25–30° | Thick for impact resistance |
| Folding knife (EDC) | 20° | General purpose |
| Hori-hori / garden blade | 15–20° | Similar to kitchen; for soil and root cutting |

**Coin Stack Reference (for consistent angle):**
- 1 coin under spine ≈ 12–15° (thin kitchen knives)
- 2 coins under spine ≈ 17–20° (general purpose)
- 3 coins under spine ≈ 22–25° (bushcraft / heavy use)
*(Varies with blade width — wider blades need more coins for the same angle.)*

## Sharpness Tests & Standards by Use

1. **Fingernail test (basic):** Lightly draw the edge across a fingernail at 45°. Sharp: catches and digs in immediately. Dull: slides across without catching.
2. **Paper test (good):** Hold a sheet of printer paper by one edge and slice downward. Sharp: cuts cleanly with no tearing. Acceptable: cuts with slight drag. Dull: tears, folds, or won't start a cut.
3. **Tomato test (kitchen standard):** Place a ripe tomato on a cutting board. Sharp: the weight of the blade alone starts the cut. Dull: requires downward pressure to break the skin.
4. **Arm hair test (very sharp):** Lightly draw the blade across arm hair without touching skin. Sharp: hair pops cleanly. Not quite: hair bends or pushes aside.
5. **Hanging hair test (razor sharp):** Hold a single hair and bring the blade to it. Razor: cuts the hair with minimal pressure (unnecessary for most bushcraft/garden use).

**Standards by use:** Bushcraft / camp knife → clean paper test; Kitchen knife → tomato test; Carving knife → arm hair test; Garden blade (hori-hori) → paper test.

## Field Expedient Sharpening & Minimum Kit

- **River stones:** Find a smooth, flat, fine-grained stone (slate, granite, basalt) with a slightly gritty feel (glassy stones won't cut fast enough). Wet the surface and use Step 4 strokes.
- **Ceramic:** Bottom unglazed ring of a ceramic mug serves as a fine hone for camp touch-ups.
- **Car window edge:** Rolled down slightly; fine-grit ceramic — 5–10 strokes per side for a quick touch-up.
- **Sandpaper on flat surface:** Place any-grit sandpaper on a flat log or rock and sharpen as on a stone.
- **Leather belt strop:** Hold taut between hand and fixed point; strop spine-first after any field sharpening.
- **Minimum recommended field kit:** Credit-card diamond plate or ceramic rod + 10 cm x 3 cm leather strop strip (fits in sheath).

## Sharpening Frequency

| Use Pattern | Recommended Frequency |
|---|---|
| Daily kitchen use | Strop daily; stone weekly |
| Weekend bushcraft | Strop before each trip; stone monthly |
| Occasional garden | Strop before use; stone seasonally |
| Heavy field use | Strop daily; stone every 2–3 days |
